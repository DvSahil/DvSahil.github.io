The idea in plain words

You do not need to know robotics or AI to follow this page. It starts with the basic idea of each part and then goes step by step to the maths and the code.

What is a drone?

An unmanned aircraft, usually a quadcopter with four propellers, flown by remote control or software. Cheap, small and capable of carrying a camera.

What is an anti-drone system?

Equipment that finds a drone, follows it, and, where the law allows, stops it. Real systems use radar, radio sensing, cameras and jammers. This project is a camera-based laboratory prototype.

What does this prototype do?

A camera watches the sky. Software finds the drone in each frame. Two motors turn the camera to keep the drone in the centre. When it is centred, a relay triggers the response module. Detect, track, neutralise.

The problem

Small drones are easy to buy and hard to watch

Drones are useful for photography, farming, mapping and delivery. The same qualities (small, fast, cheap) make unauthorised flights over airports, plants and public events a real security risk. Radar and human monitoring are expensive and miss small targets, so this project asks: can a low-cost AI camera system detect, follow and respond to a drone automatically?

System overview

The whole system on one page

This diagram redraws the thesis architecture with every arrow labelled by what it carries. Amber lines carry signals, dashed mint lines carry power, and the dotted amber loop is mechanical: the motors move the camera, so the camera sees the result of every move.

① Video

The camera sends frames to the computer over USB, about 18 to 25 per second as measured. Each frame is a 640 × 480 grid of colour pixels.

② Serial commands

Python sends short text commands such as RIGHT or FIRE over USB at 115200 baud. A 6-character command takes about half a millisecond to send.

③ Pins and actuators

Arduino pins 9 and 10 send PWM signals to the pan and tilt servos. Pin 7 switches the relay module, which switches the spark module.

④ Closed loop

The camera is mounted on the pan-tilt head. Every correction changes what the next frame shows, which changes the next error.

Chapter A

Detection: finding a drone in a picture

Detection answers two questions for every frame: is there a drone, and exactly where? Here is how, from pixels up to the finished detector.

A1 · How a computer sees

A computer does not see shapes. It sees a grid of numbers. A 640 × 480 colour frame is 307,200 pixels × 3 colours = 921,600 numbers, and at 20 frames per second that is about 18 million numbers every second. Drag the slider to change resolution and see how much detail a small drone keeps.

At low resolution a small, distant drone is only a few pixels, which is why detection range depends on camera resolution.

A2 · From numbers to objects

Older methods used hand-written rules (edges, colours, shapes). A convolutional neural network (CNN) learns the rules itself. Small filters slide over the image: early layers react to edges and corners, middle layers combine them into parts such as propeller arms, and deep layers recognise the whole drone. Nobody programs these filters. They are adjusted automatically during training until the network gets examples right.

A3 · How YOLO finds boxes in one pass

YOLO stands for You Only Look Once. Slower detectors first propose regions and then check each one. YOLO processes the whole image in a single pass and predicts many candidate boxes, each with a confidence score. Two clean-up steps follow, and you can try both below.

1 · Confidence threshold

Throw away candidates the model is not sure about. This project filters at 0.7 in the live script.

2 · Non-maximum suppression

Several boxes land on the same drone. Keep the most confident one and remove any box that overlaps it by more than the IoU limit.

IoU

Intersection over Union: overlap area divided by combined area. 1.0 is identical boxes, 0 is no overlap.

A4 · Teaching the model: training

Collect

Drone images at different angles, sizes, backgrounds and lighting.

Annotate

Draw a box around each drone in Roboflow and export in YOLO format.

Augment and split

Flip, rescale and change brightness to add variety. Split into training and validation sets.

Train

Show images, compare predictions with the true boxes, and adjust weights to reduce the loss.

Validate

Test on images the model never trained on to check it generalises.

Epoch

One full pass through the training set. The thesis reports 50 to 100 epochs.

Batch size

Images processed at once before weights update. Reported value: 16.

Overfitting

When training scores keep improving but validation scores do not: the model memorised its pictures. Validation data is the check.

Precision and recall

Precision: of the boxes drawn, how many were real drones. Recall: of the real drones, how many were found. mAP summarises both across thresholds.

Why YOLOv8 for this job

A5 · Running the detector live

model = YOLO("best.pt")                  # weights saved after training
cap = cv2.VideoCapture(0)                # USB camera, set to 640 x 480
while True:
    ok, frame = cap.read()               # one frame = 640 x 480 x 3 numbers
    results = model(frame, conf=0.7)     # inference: keep boxes with confidence >= 0.7
    for box in results[0].boxes:
        x1, y1, x2, y2 = box.xyxy[0]     # corners of the bounding box
        cx, cy = (x1 + x2) / 2, (y1 + y2) / 2   # centre: the point we track

Simplified from the repository code. The only output the rest of the system needs is the centre point (cx, cy) of the box.

A6 · What can make detection fail

Distance

A far drone covers few pixels, so there is little to recognise.

Lighting and background

Glare, dusk or a cluttered background lower confidence. Tests used moderate indoor light.

Motion blur

Fast movement smears the drone across several pixels.

Look-alikes

Birds and other flying objects can trigger false detections, which is why the confidence threshold matters.

Chapter B

Tracking: turning an error into motion

Detection gives the drone's position in the image. Tracking uses that position to move the camera so the drone returns to the centre. This is the heart of the control system.

B1 · The error

The target position is the centre of the frame. The error is how far the drone's box centre is from it, in pixels, separately for the horizontal and vertical axes.

B2 · From pixels to degrees

A motor moves in degrees, the image is measured in pixels. The link is the camera field of view. Change the camera below to see what the fixed thresholds mean physically.

B3 · The decision rules

Dead-band (30 px)

If the error is smaller than 30 px, do nothing. This stops the motors twitching on tiny errors and detector noise.

Step (2° per command)

Each command nudges the servo by 2°, about 21 px at 60° over 640 px. Small steps give smooth, controllable motion.

Lock zone (20 px)

Inside 20 px on both axes the drone counts as centred, and the system may proceed to the response.

if   error_x >  30: send("RIGHT")      # drone is right of centre: pan right
elif error_x < -30: send("LEFT")
if   error_y >  30: send("DOWN")       # y grows downward: positive means below centre
elif error_y < -30: send("UP")
if abs(error_x) < 20 and abs(error_y) < 20: send("FIRE")   # locked
A design detail worth noticing.

One 2° step is about 21 px, which is less than the 30 px dead-band half-width. So a correction can never throw the error across to the opposite side of the dead-band. In general the step in pixels must stay below twice the dead-band (here 60 px, about 5.6°) or the head will overshoot and hunt back and forth. The tuner in the simulator below lets you break this rule on purpose.

B4 · How a servo takes an angle

A hobby servo reads a pulse every 20 ms (50 Hz). The width of the pulse is the command: about 1 ms for 0°, 1.5 ms for 90°, 2 ms for 180°. Inside the servo, a small circuit compares the pulse with a position sensor and drives the motor until they match, a closed loop in miniature. The Arduino Servo library generates the pulses for you with write(angle).

MG996R servos

  • Metal-gear, high-torque hobby servo
  • Typical: about 9 to 11 kg·cm stall torque at 4.8 to 6 V
  • Typical speed: about 0.14 to 0.17 s per 60°
  • Can draw over 2 A when stalled, so it needs a proper supply, not the Arduino 5 V pin

Typical datasheet values; exact figures vary by manufacturer.

B6 · Talking to the Arduino: serial

The computer and the Arduino exchange bytes over a USB serial link at 115200 baud (about 11,500 characters a second). Python writes a text line such as RIGHT followed by a newline. The Arduino reads until the newline, compares the text, and acts.

if (Serial.available()) {
  String command = Serial.readStringUntil('\n');
  if      (command == "LEFT")  panPos  -= 2;       // 2 degrees per command
  else if (command == "RIGHT") panPos  += 2;
  else if (command == "UP")    tiltPos += 2;
  else if (command == "DOWN")  tiltPos -= 2;
  else if (command == "FIRE")  { digitalWrite(7, HIGH); delay(500); digitalWrite(7, LOW); }
  panServo.write(panPos);  tiltServo.write(tiltPos);    // pins 9 and 10
}

Chapter C

Neutralisation: from a safe signal to a switched load

This prototype has a laboratory-scale electronic response mechanism: a spark-actuated launcher module. The engineering question is how a small, delicate Arduino pin can safely trigger a circuit it could never drive directly.

Scope and responsibility.

This is a controlled-lab research model. Counter-drone measures that interfere with aircraft are regulated in most countries and are reserved for authorised agencies. This page explains the control and isolation engineering, not a deployable device.

C1 · Why not connect the load to the Arduino?

An Arduino output pin can supply only about 20 mA safely (40 mA absolute maximum) at 5 V. A relay coil needs several times that, and the response module needs a separate supply. Connecting them directly would damage the board. The answer is a switch that the Arduino controls without sharing power with the load.

C2 · How a relay works

Inside a relay is a coil and a mechanical switch. A small current through the coil makes an electromagnet that pulls the switch closed. The control side and the load side are only magnetically coupled. Typical relay modules add a transistor driver and a diode that absorbs the voltage spike when the coil switches off, which is the transistor-based switching stage described in the thesis.

1 · Command

Python sends FIRE only when both errors are under 20 px.

2 · Pin goes HIGH

The Arduino sets pin 7 high. The relay module's driver energises the coil.

3 · Contacts close

The switch connects the supply to the spark module.

4 · 500 ms, then off

delay(500) holds the pulse, then pin 7 returns LOW and the contacts open.

C3 · Safeguards in the logic

Lock first

No command is sent unless both error axes are inside the lock zone, so it only acts on a centred target.

Short pulse

The output is on for 0.5 s and then switched off, so it never stays energised.

Isolation

The relay keeps the power side electrically apart from the Arduino and the computer.

Arm switch (simulator)

The simulator has a relay-armed toggle. In any real build a physical interlock is the safer equivalent.

A limitation worth stating.

delay(500) stops the Arduino from reading new commands for half a second, so the head cannot track during the pulse. A future version would use non-blocking timing with millis() so tracking continues while the relay is on.

Honest limits and the road ahead

Limitations and future scope

Current limitations

  • Webcam range and lighting dependence
  • Indoor, small-scale laboratory testing only
  • Reduced tracking accuracy at very high drone speeds
  • Short launcher operating range
  • Relies on a stable serial link
  • No autonomous navigation or sensor fusion

Planned improvements
