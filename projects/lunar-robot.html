Lunar Self-Balancing Robot

A two-wheeled rover that stands on its wheels, a bit like a pencil balanced on a fingertip, and keeps itself upright by measuring its tilt and driving the wheels hundreds of times a second. Paired with a 6-DOF arm that collects planetary samples. Everything here runs live: push the robot and watch it recover.

the problem

Why a balancing robot needs a controller at all

Treat the robot as an inverted pendulum on a cart. Lean forward a little and gravity pulls you further forward, and the more you lean, the harder it pulls. The upright position is unstable. The only fix is to drive the wheels under the centre of mass faster than the body falls.

Equations of motion

Cart-pole model: wheel position x and body tilt θ are coupled. Accelerating the wheels is the controller's only handle on tilt.

Open-loop pole ≈ ±14 rad/s

Linearised for small angles, the model has an unstable pole near +14. That makes the time constant about 70 ms, so a 5 ms (200 Hz) loop gives roughly 14 corrections per time constant.

Lean to brake

A robot cannot just slow down: it must first lean backward. That is why the outer loop changes the angle target, not the motors directly.

Kp: the spring

Pushes harder the farther it leans. Too low: falls over. Too high: shakes.

Kd: the damper

Resists fast motion, taken from the gyro rather than a noisy derivative. Too low: oscillates. Too high: buzzes.

Why the Moon is easier to balance

Lower gravity lowers the unstable pole, so the body falls more slowly and the controller has more time. (Traction on loose regolith is the real challenge.)

sensing

Knowing the tilt: the complementary filter

The MPU6050 has two sensors that are each half-right. The accelerometer measures angle from gravity: correct on average but noisy. The gyro measures turning rate: smooth, but its tiny bias adds up to a drifting angle. A complementary filter trusts the gyro in the short term and the accelerometer in the long term. The robot uses α = 0.99.

control

The control architecture: two nested loops

Tap any block to see what it does. The fast inner loop keeps the robot upright. The slow outer loop stops it wandering. The outer loop must be much slower than the inner one, otherwise the two fight each other and oscillate.

Safety built into the firmware

Starts disabled

Motors are off at power-up and only arm when the robot is held upright.

Cut-off beyond ±35°

Past that angle recovery is impossible, so the motors stop instead of thrashing.

Command time-outs

Remote test commands expire on their own, and typing x stops everything instantly.

Anti-windup and dead-band

The integrator is clamped and the motor dead-band is compensated so small commands still move the wheels.

hardware

Hardware and bring-up

Chassis and wheels

Two driven wheels on a tall body so the centre of mass sits well above the axle.

DC motors with encoders

Encoders measure wheel speed and position for the outer loop.

MPU6050 IMU

Accelerometer plus gyro over I²C. Gyro calibrated at start-up.

TB6612FNG driver

Turns the controller's PWM signal into motor current.

2S LiPo battery

Voltage sag as it drains changes the robot's behaviour, so gains may need re-tuning.

Arduino Uno / Nano

Runs the 200 Hz loop. Gains can be tuned live over serial and saved to EEPROM.

manipulation

The 6-DOF arm for planetary sample collection

Six joints let a gripper reach any position and angle: base yaw, shoulder, elbow, wrist pitch, wrist yaw and wrist roll. Click inside the blue ring on the lunar ground (the arm's reach) to drop a sample, then press Collect. simulation

Validating in simulation first

I used CoppeliaSim, a robot simulator, to test kinematics, motion planning and autonomous control before trusting real hardware. Simulating is cheap, safe and repeatable: a bug costs seconds, not a broken gearbox.

1 · Build the scene

Robot model with joints and links, a ground plane, the sample and sensors.

2 · Kinematics

Inverse kinematics maps a target position to joint angles for the arm.

3 · Plan the motion

Waypoints and trajectories that avoid collisions and respect joint limits.

4 · Control from code

A script reads sensors and commands joints, so the same logic can later move to embedded hardware.

5 · Compare

The repository also simulates the balancing robot in Python with three controllers (tilt-only PD, cascade PID and LQR) under a 6 N push.
